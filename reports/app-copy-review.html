0 · Home

app/Home.py
headballpark
bodyStrike rate and average flatten everything. A hundred off 55 chasing 12 an over looks the same on the scorecard as a hundred off 55 on a featherbed with the game already gone. A bowler who goes at eight bowling the 18th and 20th to set batters comes out behind one who goes at eight bowling the 8th to a new pair.
bodyI'm a cricket fan who wanted numbers that account for the situation, and that don't fall apart when the sample is small. ballpark is my attempt at it, built from public ball-by-ball data (Cricsheet, 2008–2026). There is no ball-tracking here — no line and length, no field settings. What is here is the modelling: an expected-runs model that prices every ball by the state it was bowled in, a win-probability model I've checked actually holds up out of sample, and player ratings that get pulled back toward average when there isn't enough data to trust them. Roughly the questions Smart Stats and WinViz answer, from data anyone can download.
captionAbir Chakraborty · mail2abirchakraborty@gmail.com · LinkedIn · GitHub · a portfolio project, not a product ← name, email, LinkedIn, GitHub
metricdeliveries modelled — 295,732 · caption: 1,243 matches, 2008–2026
metricwin-prob Brier (test) — 0.182 · −0.008 vs required-rate model · caption: 2nd-innings AUC 0.88 · calibration error 0.059
metricxRuns error (walk-forward) — 1.682 · −0.012 vs over×wickets average · caption: runs/ball · runs −0.056 low, on purpose (see model card)

buttonWhere to look first
body1. Match replay — pick a game, watch the win-probability line swing, see the projected first-innings total narrow ball by ball, and the deliveries that turned it.
body2. Players — the same leaderboard raw and shrunk, side by side. Watch the small-sample names slide back toward the pack when you switch it on.
body3. Matchups — pull up a "he can't play the leggie" reputation and see how much of it holds up once you account for how few balls it's built on.
body4. Tactics — a bowling-change optimiser run on real death overs. Mostly it does what the captain did. The interesting part is when it doesn't.
body5. Model card — where the models hold up, where they don't, and what a public dataset can't see.

The point was to get the method right: check the models season by season the way you'd actually retrain them, calibrate the probabilities, and regress the small samples. The last section of the model card is what I'd want to build first if I had tracking data to work with.

3 · Matchups

app/pages/3_Matchups.py
headMatchups
blurbHow a batter goes against a type of bowling — right-arm pace, the leggie, left-arm orthodox, and so on. Where a batter has faced a type only a few dozen times, his personal record against it is regressed hard toward how batters like him generally do, so the number isn't just noise.
bodyTake every batter–bowling-type pairing with at least 40 balls behind it — 692 of them. The average raw gap from what you'd expect is 39 runs per 100 balls. Once you regress each one for how little it's built on, that drops to 8 — about 19% of it. Most of what gets called a matchup on TV is a small sample doing the talking.
controlBatter dropdown, default Kohli
charthorizontal bars per bowling type, x-axis "expected runs per 100 balls (shrunk, with 95% range)"
tablebowling type · balls · prior /100 · shrunk /100 · his edge /100 · just this matchup /100
captionprior is where you'd put him against this type with no personal record to go on — his overall level, plus how batters generally do against it. his edge is how much better or worse than that the model has him. just this matchup is the slice of that which is specific to him against this bowling — the bit that gets regressed hardest, and it's usually small.

4 · Tactics

app/pages/4_Tactics.py
headTactics — bowling-change optimiser
blurbSecond innings, tight finishes. From a given point in the chase, the optimiser tries every legal way to bowl out the rest of the innings — who bowls which over, within the quotas — and picks the one that gives the chasing side the worst chance. Shown next to what the captain actually did, and what the difference between the two was worth.
bodyRun on every tight finish in the data, the optimiser's plan and the one the captain actually used come out within two win-probability points of each other {NN}% of the time. At the death the gap between a front-line over and a fifth bowler's is only three or four runs, and spread over the last few overs that usually doesn't add up to much. The rows below are where the two disagree most. Even the largest gap is about {NN} points, and it's the model preferring one attack to another — not a single over swinging the game.
tablelabel · from over · runs needed · balls left · captain · optimiser · the captain's call gave the batting side · won
controlTake a closer look at one dropdown, defaults to KKR v SRH 2023 (biggest disagreement)
captionStart of the 15th over: 124/4, chasing 172, 48 needed off 36. Kolkata Knight Riders won. (example values)
button"The optimiser's call" / "What the captain did"
metricchasing side to win — {NN}% · caption "projects the chase to about {score}, taken as a spread rather than a fixed number" · lift caption "About {NN} points more than the optimiser's plan — what bowling it this way was worth to the batting side."
buttonThe five it rated best:
captionScoring an allocation: each remaining over gets the expected runs and wickets for the match state, nudged by how much better or worse than average that bowler is — the shrunk rating from the Players tab — with a small extra cost for spin in the 19th and 20th, which goes for about a run an over more than pace there in the data. That gives a projected total, which is then treated as a range rather than a single number — a chase can land a couple of overs' worth of runs either side of the projection — and the win probability is averaged over that range. Quotas and no back-to-back overs are kept to. It doesn't know who's on strike or where the field is.

5 · Model card

app/pages/5_Model_card.py
headModel card
blurbThe part where I say where the models fall short. Most cricket models don't, and I think that's a mistake.
buttonHow the models were checked
bodySeason by season. For each season, the model is trained on every season before it and scored on that one — the way you'd actually retrain it if it were in use. Nothing is scored on data it was trained on.
bodyA frozen split (train up to 2021, test 2024–26) is kept separately and looked at once, as a check on how badly a model goes stale if you don't retrain it.
bodyLeakage is checked by code, not by reading it back: one test rebuilds the per-ball features from a cut-short innings and checks the rows that survive are byte-for-byte identical; another checks no match ends up in two different splits.
buttonLayer 1 — expected runs and wickets
metricxRuns error (runs/ball) 1.682 · runs below actual −0.056 · wicket log loss 0.194
bodyIt only just beats a decent conditional average, and that's expected — a single ball is a 0, a 4 or a wicket, so most of the error is just noise you can't model away. xRuns isn't meant to call the next ball; it's meant to be an unbiased yardstick everything else is measured against. One known problem: because it only ever trains on past seasons, it runs about 0.056 runs a ball below what actually happens — it can't see this year's scoring going up. That's a roughly flat offset, so it moves everyone's number by the same amount and doesn't change the order. But it's there, and I'd rather flag it than quietly correct it.
buttonLayer 2 — win probability
metricBrier, test seasons 0.182 · 2nd-innings AUC 0.876 · calibration error, all balls 0.023
bodyPut plainly: a run chase is mostly about the run rate. A plain regression on required rate, wickets in hand and balls left is already a strong model, and on the untouched 2024–26 seasons it's about level with the fancier gradient-boosted one on second-innings balls — a touch ahead on log loss. The Impact Player rule and deeper batting line-ups have if anything made chases more rate-driven.
bodySo the tree only gets a fifth of the weight in the blend. It earns that on the things the regression can't do: the first innings, the projected-total fan on the replay page, keeping the probabilities calibrated across the phases, and producing the ball-by-ball swings the Players tab adds up. Calibration is done on held-out predictions, never on a tail of recent seasons — one season is about 70 games, nowhere near enough to fit a calibration curve.
buttonLayer 3 — player impact
bodyThe shrunk numbers are ridge-regression coefficients on runs above expected — which is the same thing as putting a prior on every player centred on average, with the amount of shrinkage picked by cross-validation. 844 players get a number. The 95% ranges come from resampling whole matches, not individual balls, because balls in the same match aren't independent.
captiontable captions: "Raw numbers flatter them most" / "Raw numbers sell them shortest"
buttonLayer 4 — matchups
metricof a raw matchup split, this much is real — 19% · raw 39 → shrunk 8 runs per 100
bodyAbout four-fifths of a raw batter-vs-type split is small-sample wobble. The type-level effects are real — off-spin goes for about 5 runs/100 less than average, the leggie for about 6 more — but the personal matchup, once you account for how thin it is, is usually not much.
buttonWhat it can't see, and what I've left out
bodyNo ball-tracking. No line, no length, no pace off the pitch, no revs, no field settings, no pitch map. "The leggie" here is a stand-in for what Hawk-Eye would tell you directly.
bodyBowling styles aren't in the data. I typed them in by hand for the ~320 most-used players — enough to cover ~90% of balls faced and ~87% of balls bowled. The rest fall back to the type-level number.
bodyThe simulator treats bowling as average unless you give it a plan, doesn't track who's on strike ball to ball, and skips byes and wides off the bat.
bodyThe optimiser rolls forward expected values rather than simulating, and can't see the field or which batters are in.
bodySuper overs, no-results and DLS first innings are left out of training. Four overs in IPL history have seven legal balls (umpires miscounting) — those are kept as they are.
bodyIPL only. Adding another league is a one-line config change and a download, but I haven't retrained or re-checked anything on the BBL, PSL or internationals.
buttonWhat I'd build first with ball-tracking data
body1. Cluster deliveries by where they pitch and how fast, and rate batters against those clusters — instead of "the leggie", the actual ball. The matchup model is starved of the one thing that makes a matchup real.
body2. A shot-quality model. xRuns that also knew the false-shot rate, whether the batter was beaten, how well he middled it. That would tell a batter riding his luck apart from one in complete control — the single biggest source of noise left in the player numbers.
body3. Fielding. The data here only catches the wicket-ending stuff. Range, boundaries saved, the pressure a gun fielder puts on — badly under-measured, and probably the quickest thing to get right.
body4. How a bowler fades within a spell — pace dropping off over to over, which the model barely touches right now.

headFull-match simulator
blurbThe full IPL ball-by-ball history is loaded for you; set two line-ups and a venue and it plays the game out ball by ball. Each player's profile is his own recent form, weighted toward strong opposition; the ball outcome mixes the batter and the bowler, adjusts for who's set and how the last few overs have gone, and the win probability at the end is calibrated. Add another league's Cricsheet zip, or remove a set, and everything refits.
body📦 A separate tool from the pages above — those use models I trained offline on the IPL; this one builds everything on the fly from whatever data is loaded, entirely in your browser. The IPL set comes preloaded; any other men's T20 zip from cricsheet.org can be added below. It scrolls inside its own frame.
controlLoad match data — preloaded dataset row with × to remove; drop zone / file picker to add more (multiple, .zip)
presetOpens on Gujarat Titans vs Royal Challengers Bengaluru at Narendra Modi Stadium, Ahmedabad, 10,000 simulations — all still editable
