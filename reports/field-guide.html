Tab 00 · the landing page

Home

Four numbers, one per model layer, each next to the simple thing it has to beat. If the modelling doesn't hold up here, nothing else in the app is worth much.

Metric

Win‑prob Brier (test) — 0.182 −0.008 vs the required‑run‑rate baseline

What it shows
How wrong the win‑probability model is, on average, on matches it never saw during training (the 2024–26 seasons). Squared error between the probability it announced and what actually happened. Lower is better; 0 is a perfect oracle, 0.25 is a coin that always says 50%.
Why it's useful
A win-probability number is only worth putting on screen if it's calibrated — when it says 70%, the side should win about 70% of the time. Brier rewards being confident and right. I'm showing the −0.008 gap over the baseline (a regression on just required rate, wickets and balls) because it's small: a recent chase is mostly a rate problem, and the fancier model only just clears the simple one.
Brier score — worked on four balls
# Brier = mean over test balls of (predicted_prob − outcome)²
# outcome = 1 if the batting/chasing side went on to win, 0 if it lost, 0.5 for a tie

  ball   predicted   outcome   squared error
   A       0.90        1         (0.90 − 1)²  = 0.010
   B       0.60        0         (0.60 − 0)²  = 0.360
   C       0.30        0         (0.30 − 0)²  = 0.090
   D       0.55        1         (0.55 − 1)²  = 0.203
                                          ─────────
                              mean over 4 balls = 0.166

Across all 51,563 test‑season balls that mean is 0.182; the RRR baseline scores 0.190.
Metric

xRuns RMSE (walk‑forward) — 1.682 −0.012 vs "average runs by over × wickets"

What it shows
Typical error, in runs, when the expected‑runs model predicts how many runs the next ball is worth. It is evaluated walk‑forward: trained on every prior season, scored on the next one, repeated.
Why it's useful
One ball is a 0, a 4 or a wicket, so the error is always going to be big. xRuns isn't meant to call the next ball. It's meant to be a yardstick with no bias in it, that every player number is then measured against. Only just beating a good conditional average is the result you want here, not a worry.
Root‑mean‑square error — worked on three balls
# RMSE = sqrt( mean( (xRuns_ball − actual_runs_off_bat)² ) )

  ball   xRuns   actual   error²
   A     1.30      0      (1.30)²  = 1.69
   B     1.40      4      (2.60)²  = 6.76
   C     1.50      1      (0.50)²  = 0.25
                          ────────
              mean = 2.90 ;  sqrt(2.90) = 1.70

Over all 172,684 out‑of‑sample balls this is 1.682. The mean of xRuns
also lands 0.056 runs/ball below the mean actual — a model trained on the
past always trails a rising‑scoring league. That bias is constant, so it
shifts every player's number by the same amount and leaves the rankings intact.
Metric

2nd‑innings AUC — 0.88  ·  ECE, all balls — 0.023

What they show
AUC — pick a random ball from a match the chasing side won and a random ball from one they lost; AUC is the chance the model gave the winning‑side ball the higher win probability. 0.5 is a coin, 1.0 is perfect ranking. ECE (expected calibration error) — bucket every ball by the probability the model announced, and measure how far the announced probability sits from the actual win rate in that bucket.
Why it's useful
AUC tells you the model ranks situations in the right order. ECE tells you the number itself can be read as a probability. Both matter. An ECE of 0.023 means that across the whole app, when the line says a number, it's about 2 points off the truth on average.
Expected calibration error — one bucket
# ECE = Σ over 20 buckets of  (balls in bucket / total balls) × | mean predicted − actual win rate |

  bucket [0.60, 0.65):   9,900 balls,  mean prediction 0.62,  actual win rate 0.596
     contribution = (9,900 / 344,000) × |0.62 − 0.596|
                  = 0.0288 × 0.024  =  0.00069

Sum that over all 20 buckets → 0.023.
Metric

Matchup shrinkage ratio — 0.19

What it shows
How much of a raw "this batter vs this kind of bowling" split survives once it is regressed for sample size. Averaged over the 692 batter × bowler‑archetype cells that have at least 40 balls.
Why it's useful
It's the main thing that came out of the project, in one number. TV quotes 40-ball matchup splits as if they mean something; four-fifths of that is the small sample talking.
The ratio
  mean | raw split |          over 692 cells  =  38.9  runs per 100 balls
  mean | shrunk matchup delta |               =   7.5  runs per 100 balls
                                                 ─────
                              ratio  =  7.5 / 38.9  =  0.19
Tab 01 · one match, ball by ball

Match replay

Pick a match. Three things: the win-probability lines over both innings with wickets marked, the projected first-innings total as a fan, and a table of the deliveries that moved it most.

Chart

Win‑probability lines y = P(win) · x = ball number · one line per side

What it shows
Two lines that add to 100% — each side's probability of winning, recalculated after every legal ball. A dotted line at 50%, a vertical rule at the innings break, and a circle at every wicket (hover for the batsman and how he went — "c Patidar b Hazlewood"). The line for first‑innings balls is the model's win_prob directly; for the chase it is 1 − win_prob, because win_prob always refers to the side currently batting and in the chase that's the other team.
Why it's useful
You can see which over a match turned, and by how much. It's also what every "wins added" number in the Players tab is built from.
How win_prob is computed (a chase ball)
Features: required run rate, runs required, balls remaining, wickets in hand, current run rate, rate pressure, over, striker balls faced, partnership balls, target, venue, phase. Two models are run and blended, then a calibration curve is applied.
The blend and the calibration — for the last ball of that match
# State: CSK need 2 off 1, 3 wickets in hand (7 down), chasing 162.
required_run_rate = 2 / 1 × 6 = 12.0

# 1. gradient‑boosted tree (15 leaves, very shrunk)  → p_tree  ≈ 0.39
# 2. logistic on [req_rate, wickets, balls, req_rate×wickets, ...] → p_logit ≈ 0.43

blended   = 0.2 × p_tree + 0.8 × p_logit
          = 0.2 × 0.39 + 0.8 × 0.43        = 0.422

calibrated = isotonic_map(0.422)            = 0.418   # P(CSK win) before the ball

# The ball is a run‑out. CSK 160/8, 2 needed off 0 balls → chase failed.
P(CSK win) after the ball                   = 0.000
Chart

Projected first‑innings score fan of five quantiles vs the actual line

What it shows
At every ball of the first innings, where the final total is likely to land. The dark band is the 30–70% range, the light band 10–90%, the solid line the median projection, and the black line the score as it actually accrued.
Why it's useful
It's the clearest thing in the app — the fan narrows as the innings settles, and a partnership or a collapse visibly shifts the whole range.
How it's computed
Five separate LightGBM regressors, trained with a quantile loss at α = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9. Each predicts runs still to come from the current state (not the final total — bounded below by zero, and it spares the model from re‑learning the current score every ball). The five outputs are sorted per row so the bands can't cross.
Worked at over 10.1 — score 47
# five quantile models predict "remaining runs" from the current state:
   q10  →  51        q30 →  59        q50 →  71        q70 →  86        q90 → 102

projected total at quantile q  =  current_score + max(0, remaining_runs_q)

   q10 total = 47 + 51 =  98
   q30 total = 47 + 59 = 106
   q50 total = 47 + 71 = 118      # the median line
   q70 total = 47 + 86 = 133
   q90 total = 47 +102 = 149

# 80% of the model's mass for the final total sits between 98 and 149.

Tab 02 · who is actually good

Players

Two numbers per player. Wins added is what happened over a season. Shrunk true rate is the skill read — how well he bats or bowls once 40 balls stops counting as a sample. A batting/bowling toggle and a minimum-balls slider filter the list.

Table + toggle

Raw vs shrunk runs per 100 balls above expectation

What it shows
Per player: their raw scoring rate, the rate an average batter would be expected to manage in the same situations (from xRuns), the gap between them (the "naive" number), and then the shrunk estimate with a 95% interval. The toggle flips the leaderboard between the naive gap and the shrunk estimate.
Why it's useful
The naive gap is roughly what a stats site would give you, and it's mostly noise for anyone with a short career. The shrunk number is the one you'd actually back.
player (bat)ballsraw /100expected /100naive gapshrunk /10095% interval
SS Iyer2,833132.9129.7+3.2+9.7[+0.5, +20.2]
RG Sharma3,032130.1127.5+2.6+2.0[−6.5, +11.0]

Every column — worked for SS Iyer (2,833 balls, 3,765 runs)
raw / 100        =  runs / balls × 100          =  3765 / 2833 × 100   = 132.9

expected / 100   =  (Σ xRuns over his balls) / balls × 100
                 =  3674.8 / 2833 × 100                              = 129.7

naive gap / 100  =  raw − expected  =  132.9 − 129.7                 = +3.2

# shrunk: one big ridge regression over every ball in the data.
#   residualᵢ = actual_runsᵢ − xRunsᵢ
#   Xᵢ = a row with a 1 in the striker's column and a 1 in the bowler's column
#   minimise  Σ wᵢ (residualᵢ − Xᵢβ)²  +  α Σ βⱼ²      with α = 100 (chosen by 5-fold CV)

β for "SS Iyer, batting"   =  +0.097 runs/ball
shrunk / 100               =  0.097 × 100                            = +9.7

# Why +9.7 is ABOVE the naive +3.2 here:
# the bowler columns are in the same regression, so the fit also nets out
# opponent quality. Iyer faced tougher-than-average bowling; the joint model
# gives that back to him. For a short career the α penalty dominates instead
# and pulls the estimate toward zero (see the funnel in the Model card).

95% interval  =  2.5th … 97.5th percentile of β across 60 refits, each
                 resampling whole matches with replacement            = [+0.5, +20.2]
The shrink, in isolation — a hypothetical 60‑ball player
raw gap        =  +20 runs / 100 balls        # off just 60 balls — almost meaningless

# an L2 penalty of strength α acts like an empirical-Bayes prior centred on 0.
# for a lone one-hot column the coefficient is pulled toward 0 by ≈ n / (n + α):

shrunk gap     ≈  60 / (60 + 100)  ×  20      =  0.375 × 20   = +7.5

# a 3,000-ball player:  3000 / (3000 + 100) × gap  ≈  0.968 × gap  — barely moves.

Raw vs shrunk scatter x = naive gap · y = shrunk · bubble = career balls

What it shows
Every qualifying player as a bubble. The dashed diagonal is "no shrinkage at all" (y = x). A player sitting well below the line is flattered by their raw numbers; well above, sold short. The biggest movers are labelled — those are your over‑ and under‑rated lists.

Tab 03 · batter vs a kind of bowling

Matchups

Kohli vs Rashid is 90 balls. A right-hander vs right-arm wrist-spin in the middle overs is thousands. This tab works in the second language, and regresses the batter-specific part of a matchup toward zero, so a thin split falls back to how batters like him generally do.

Headline stat

Four-fifths of a raw matchup split is small-sample noise

What it shows
Across 692 batter × archetype cells with ≥40 balls, the average raw deviation from expectation is 38.9 runs/100; the shrunk matchup delta averages 7.5, and the truly batter‑specific piece (the interaction term) averages just 3.6.
Why it's useful
The number to have when someone says "he can't play the leggie". The type-level effects hold up — off-spin does go for less, leg-spin does cost more — but the personal matchup, once you account for how thin it is, is small.
Chart

Expected runs per 100 by archetype shrunk, with a 95% interval whisker

What it shows
For the selected batter, one horizontal bar per bowler archetype (right‑arm pace, left‑arm pace, off‑spin, leg‑spin, left‑arm orthodox, left‑arm wrist‑spin), giving the runs per 100 balls the model expects them to score against that kind of bowling, with an uncertainty whisker.
Why it's useful
A sample-corrected read on a matchup, with the range attached — and for a thin one the range is wide enough to say "we don't really know".
How it's built
The same ridge idea as Layer 3, with a third block of columns: one per batter × archetype pair. The interaction columns have few balls each, so the α penalty pushes them toward zero and the prediction falls back to the archetype prior.
The decomposition — SS Iyer vs right‑arm pace (1,179 balls)
expected_runs_per_100  =  ( context  +  batter effect  +  archetype effect  +  interaction ) × 100

   context      =  mean xRuns over the balls he faced vs right‑arm pace   =  1.308 /ball  → 130.8
   batter effect (β for "SS Iyer")                                        =  +0.077 /ball →  +7.7
   archetype effect (β for "right‑arm pace"; off‑spin is −5.0, leg‑spin +6.3)  = +0.002  →  +0.2
   interaction  (β for "SS Iyer × right‑arm pace" — the shrunk matchup)   =  +0.024 /ball →  +2.4
                                                                                            ─────
   expected_runs_per_100  =  130.8 + 7.7 + 0.2 + 2.4                                      =  141.1

   archetype_prior_per_100  =  130.8 + 7.7 + 0.2   (no batter-specific info)              =  138.7
   matchup_delta_per_100    =  7.7 + 0.2 + 2.4     (total lift over raw context)          =  +10.3
   interaction_per_100      =  2.4                 (the part that is really "this batter") =  +2.4

   95% interval on the matchup  =  [ −6.4 , +23.4 ]   →  not distinguishable from zero.
Tab 04 · the bowling‑change optimiser

Tactics

Second innings, tight finishes. From a point in the chase, given each bowler's remaining quota, the optimiser tries every legal way to bowl out the innings and picks the one that gives the chasing side the worst odds. Shown next to what the captain actually did.

Headline stat

The optimiser and the captain land within two points 84% of the time

What it shows
Run over all 1,180 close‑finish states in the data, the optimiser's plan and the captain's real one come out within two win‑probability points of each other 84% of the time, within five points 90%. Eight finishes show a gap above ten points; one above fifteen.
Why it's useful
Bowler-quality edges at the death are real but small, so a tool that disagreed with the captain every time would just be wrong. The handful of real disagreements are the model preferring one attack to another, not one over swinging the game.
Table + inspector

Optimiser vs captain Δ = captain's projected win prob − optimiser's

What it shows
Ranked by how much win probability the captain's real choice handed the batting side versus the optimiser's pick. The inspector below expands one state: the two allocations, their projected win probabilities and final scores, and the top five allocations the search considered.
Why it's useful
It puts a number on a second-guess — "that over cost us X points" — and shows how close the alternatives were, so you can judge it yourself.
The expected‑value rollout — Sunrisers chasing Kolkata, 2023 (the biggest disagreement in the data)
# State at the start of over 15: SRH 124/4, chasing 172 → 48 needed off 36 (6 overs).
# For each remaining over, given the state (score s, wickets w, balls b):

    runs_this_over   =  6 × xRuns(state)  −  bowler_effect × 6      # Layer 1 minus the bowler's Layer-3 edge
    wkts_this_over   =  6 × xWicket(state)
    s ← s + max(runs_this_over, 0) ;   w ← w + wkts_this_over ;   b ← b + 6

# bowler_effect (runs saved per 100 balls, shrunk Layer-3 estimate):
#   SP Narine  +28   ·   CV Varun  +16   ·   AS Roy  +12   ·   VG Arora  −1   ·   SN Thakur  −4
# plus: a spinner's 19th costs +0.5, the 20th +0.8 — spin goes for more there.

  ── CAPTAIN:  Thakur → Varun → Arora → Varun → Arora → Varun ──
    seam from Thakur and Arora around Varun          projected SRH total  ≈ 184

  ── OPTIMISER:  Varun → Narine → Varun → Narine → Varun → Arora ──
    spin through the middle overs, a seamer at the 20th  projected SRH total  ≈ 175

# ~9 runs across the six overs, on the model's read of KKR's spin vs their seam.

  the projected total is a mean, not a fact — treat it as Normal(mean, σ),
  σ = 2.85 × √(balls left) ≈ 17 with 36 to go, and average Layer 2 over it:

    captain    P(SRH reach 172 | mean 184, σ 17)   →  chasing-side win prob  ≈ 0.79
    optimiser  P(SRH reach 172 | mean 175, σ 17)   →  chasing-side win prob  ≈ 0.61
                                                                        Δ  =  +0.18

# Without the σ step, Layer 2 at the end of an innings is a step at the target:
# a two-run change in the projection would swing this 60 points, not 18. The
# search enumerates every legal ordering (≤4 overs each, no back-to-back) and
# keeps the lowest chasing-side win probability.
Tab 05 · where the models are wrong

Model card

The tab where I say where the models fall short. How they were checked, then a section per layer with its figure and its number, then the limitations and what I'd build first with ball-tracking data. These are the figures the app ships.

Figure · Layer 1

xRuns vs actual, by phase

What it shows
Mean expected runs per ball against mean actual runs per ball, split by powerplay / middle / death, on out‑of‑sample balls. The bars should match within each phase.
Why it's useful
A point predictor of one ball is hopeless, but the averages need to line up or every downstream player number inherits a phase bias. They line up to within a few hundredths.
Figure · Layer 2

Win‑probability calibration by phase

What it shows
Predicted win probability on the x‑axis, observed win rate on the y‑axis, one line per phase of the second innings. The dashed diagonal is perfect calibration.
Why it's useful
This is the plot that matters most for a win‑probability model. If the death‑overs line sagged below the diagonal, the ribbon would be systematically overconfident exactly when it matters most. It tracks the diagonal within about 3 points in every phase.
Reading one point
# A point at (predicted 0.70, observed 0.68) means:
# of all the balls where the model said "70% chance the chase succeeds",
# the chase actually succeeded 68% of the time. Close to the diagonal = trustworthy.
Figure · Layer 2

Reliability — over/under‑confidence by bucket

What it shows
The same information as the calibration curve, drawn as observed minus predicted per probability bucket. Bars above the zero line = the model was under‑confident there; below = over‑confident.
Why it's useful
It makes small, systematic miscalibration visible — a curve that hugs the diagonal can still have a consistent 3‑point lean, and this plot shows it.
Figure · Layer 3

How much "above expectation" survives shrinkage — batters

What it shows
Naive gap (raw − expected) on the x‑axis, shrunk effect on the y‑axis, one point per batter with 200+ balls, bubble size = balls. The dashed line is y = x.
Why it's useful
The players furthest off the diagonal are exactly the ones a raw leaderboard misranks.
Figure · Layer 3

The same, for bowlers

What it shows
Bowlers' naive gap vs shrunk effect. Bumrah is the outlier: a raw economy that looks ordinary, a shrunk estimate that rates him the best in the data, because he bowls the hardest overs to the best batters and the joint fit gives that back to him.
Why it's useful
It shows the correction is not just "shrink toward zero" — for high‑volume bowlers it is a re‑attribution of opponent and situation difficulty.
Bumrah — why raw and shrunk disagree so hard
raw / 100        =  3886 conceded / 3379 balls × 100                =  115.0   # ~6.9 an over — ordinary
expected / 100   =  (Σ xRuns over his balls) / balls × 100          =  134.3   # an average bowler here → ~8.1 an over
naive gap        =  115.0 − 134.3                                   =  −19.3   # already good: 19 fewer than expected

shrunk (runs saved / 100)  =  −β × 100  =  +36.1   95% interval [+29.8, +41.7]
# the joint ridge also strips out the quality of the batters he bowled to →
# the estimate nearly doubles. Tight interval = high confidence.
Figure · Layer 3

Shrinkage funnel

What it shows
Career balls on the x‑axis (capped at 6,000), runs/100 above expectation on the y. The red cloud is the naive number, the green the shrunk. At the left edge — short careers — the red spread is huge and the green collapses to a thread.
Why it's useful
It is shrinkage made visual: the estimate is only allowed to be extreme when the sample earns it.
Text

Validation protocol · limitations · "what I'd build with tracking data"

What it shows
Walk‑forward is primary: for each season, train on every prior season and score it — the way a deployed model is actually retrained. A frozen 2024–26 split is kept as a drift stress‑test, touched once. Leakage is a mechanical test: rebuild the feature table from a truncated innings and assert the surviving rows are bit‑identical.
Why it's useful
Publishing the failure modes — no ball tracking, 79% matchup coverage, the xRuns bias, a simulator that runs ~10% low — is the strongest possible signal that the numbers elsewhere in the app can be trusted.

Formula index

Every formula in one place

ECE                   Σ_bins (nᵦ/N) · | mean pᵦ − mean yᵦ |
AUC                   P( p(random winner ball) > p(random loser ball) )

WPA                   win_prob_after − win_prob_before      # batting side's frame
leverage              rolling mean over last 6 balls of | Δ win_prob |
wins_added(season)    Σ WPA over the player's balls        # × −1 for bowlers, × −0.5 for fielders

raw / 100             runs / balls × 100
expected / 100        (Σ xRuns) / balls × 100
naive gap             raw/100 − expected/100
shrunk effect (β)     argmin_β  Σ wᵢ (residualᵢ − Xᵢβ)² + α Σ βⱼ²      residual = actual − xRuns
                      α chosen by 5-fold match-grouped CV  (Layer 3: α = 100)

matchup expected/100  ( context + β_batter + β_archetype + β_interaction ) × 100  # Layer 4a

EV rollout (over)     runs = 6·xRuns(state) − bowler_effect·6 ;  wkts = 6·xWicket(state)   # Layer 4c
